# Company brief, content, and evidence

## Information that changes decisions

First extract answers from the available materials. A brief may come from a
conversation, documents, an existing site, or a form. Record sources, fact status,
and asset permissions where known; internal notes do not need to appear in the
website interface.

| Information | Effect on the website | If unavailable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name, industry, location/service area | Identity and business context | Ask before writing specific claims |
| Audience and main goal | Message, navigation, CTA | Propose assumptions from the brief and flag them for confirmation |
| Services and client deliverables | Service sections | Ask about core services; do not invent capabilities |
| Differentiators and supporting evidence | Strengths and credibility | Describe the capabilities already supported by information |
| Projects, documentation, results | Portfolio | Omit or use labeled examples if requested |
| Contacts and CTA destinations | Client inquiry path | Build the layout; do not activate fictitious destinations |
| Logo, colors, fonts, photos | Visual identity | Propose a visual direction; a temporary wordmark can be used in a draft |
| Language, CMS, stack, hosting | Architecture and maintenance | Follow the project; choose a simple implementation for a new site |

Initial questions usually only need to cover the company's industry, whom it
wants to reach, its main services, and what visitors should do. Ask about assets
and technical details only when they affect the work.

## Facts and evidence

- Do not invent founding dates, client counts, experience, revenue, project
  results, team positions, addresses, phone numbers, certifications, or testimonials.
- Claims such as "best," "number one," and "certified" need evidence. Use accurate
  descriptions of capabilities when that evidence is unavailable.
- Clarify attribution: projects completed by a founder before the company existed
  are not automatically company projects. Describe them as team member experience
  if the user confirms the attribution and publication is permitted.
- Use client logos, photos of people, testimonials, and project materials according
  to known permissions. If permission is unclear, use an anonymous description
  or omit the asset; ask only when it is needed for the requested outcome.
- Stock or generated images may serve as illustrations when suitable and their
  usage rights are clear. Do not present them as photos of the actual office,
  team, factory, or projects. Add labels or captions when the context could mislead.
- When using public sources, prioritize official company sources and keep reference
  links. Flag conflicting information for confirmation. Web research is not
  mandatory when user materials are sufficient; verify current claims through
  accessible sources when needed.
- Use sample data in drafts only when the user requests a demo or example, with
  visible labels. Avoid lorem ipsum for the main message.

## Choosing the structure

Use one page when services are concise and evidence is limited. Use multiple
pages when services need individual explanations, projects have case studies,
multiple languages are needed, or the user requests them. Do not split every
section into a thin page simply to increase the page count.

An adaptable starting pattern: introduction -> services -> evidence/portfolio ->
about/process -> contact. Sites that rely on institutional credibility may
introduce the company earlier. Optional sections should have a concrete purpose.

## Writing content

- Introduction: identify the company, core services, audience, and an appropriate
  CTA. A slogan may accompany a concrete explanation but should not replace it.
- Services: describe customer needs, scope, deliverables, and the next step.
  Match the level of detail to the audience's knowledge.
- Strengths: explain the mechanism or evidence; avoid strings of adjectives.
- Portfolio: include the project name/category, need, company scope and role,
  solution, documentation, and results. If numeric results are unavailable,
  explain confirmed deliverables or benefits without inventing metrics.
- About: provide context and capabilities that help prospective clients decide.
  Add history, mission and vision, and team listings only when relevant and available.
- Contact: use a clear purpose, correct channels, service area when relevant,
  and the information needed to start a conversation. Do not promise an
  unconfirmed response time.

New companies can demonstrate their process, team capabilities, or clearly
labeled conceptual projects. Do not conceal a lack of experience with fictitious
testimonials or client lists.

For bilingual sites, keep content and link destinations equivalent. Do not add
a language switcher that does not change the content.
